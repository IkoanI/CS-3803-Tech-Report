Methodology

To evaluate the usability of the Korean Language Game, we conducted a mixed-methods usability study involving a total of 6 participants. The participants primarily consisted of beginner Korean learners, as well as individuals with informal exposure to the language through media such as K-dramas. This demographic was selected to reflect the platform’s intended user base of novice learners who require intuitive navigation and supportive learning tools.

The usability study consisted of three main phases: pre-test, task-based testing, and post-test evaluation. During the pre-test phase, participants were asked a set of background questions to assess their prior experience with Korean, their learning goals, and their familiarity with other language-learning applications. These questions provided important context for interpreting user behavior and expectations during the testing process.

Participants were then asked to complete a series of seven representative tasks designed to simulate real user interactions with the platform. These tasks included creating an account, locating developer information, studying vocabulary through flashcards, playing vocabulary games, checking progress metrics, submitting feedback, and logging out. The sessions were conducted both remotely and in person and were recorded for analysis, allowing us to observe user interactions and identify usability issues.

Following task completion, participants responded to a set of post-test questions to evaluate their experience with navigation, task difficulty, and overall usability. These responses provided insight into both the most problematic and most enjoyable aspects of the platform. Additionally, task completion times were recorded for each participant, enabling comparison across tasks and identification of areas requiring improvement. By combining both quantitative performance data and qualitative feedback, this methodology allowed us to develop a comprehensive understanding of the platform’s usability strengths and weaknesses.

Findings

Our quantitative data revealed significant discrepancies in task completion times. Task 4 consistently took the longest across all participants, with completion times ranging from 20 seconds to a peak of 136 seconds[cite: 277]. Conversely, Task 5 was completed rapidly, averaging under 10 seconds[cite: 277].

Qualitative feedback highlighted major navigation and discoverability issues[cite: 62]. Multiple users reported a lack of clarity in tasks[cite: 63], particularly regarding how to locate specific study materials like flashcards versus games.

During the "Red Light Green Light" game module, participants experienced notable input frustration[cite: 133]. The system exhibited a lack of learning support [cite: 129] and unclear answer expectations[cite: 130], leading to user errors.

Users also expressed difficulty navigating the initial Units page. The linear layout forced unnecessary scrolling, and the lack of English instructions alienated true beginners who were not yet familiar with the Korean alphabet[cite: 277].

Finally, the existing feedback mechanism was deeply flawed. One participant reported that attempting to submit feedback caused the entire website to crash[cite: 277], highlighting a critical functional error.

Analysis

As seen in the data visualizations above, the extended completion times for Task 4 directly correlate with the input frustration observed during gameplay[cite: 133]. Screen recordings and feedback revealed that the game's text entry was strictly case-sensitive and lacked integrated Korean keyboard support[cite: 277]. Because the system expected exact matches without providing technical input assistance, users spent excess time trying to format their answers rather than learning the language.

The discoverability issues reported by users stem from a fragmented information architecture. As Participant 4 noted, the flashcards and vocabulary games were housed on different pages, making the interface counterintuitive[cite: 277]. This unnecessary separation forces users to execute additional clicks to navigate between related study modes, slowing down the learning loop.

Furthermore, the absence of English translations on the primary navigation screens creates an immediate barrier to entry. Beginners cannot efficiently navigate a site written entirely in the language they are actively trying to learn, emphasizing the critical need for bilingual UI elements to support early-stage learners[cite: 277].

The crash experienced during the feedback task [cite: 277] indicates a lack of structured data handling on the backend. A generic, open-ended text box is insufficient for capturing actionable bug reports or user suggestions, resulting in poor system stability.

Despite these technical and navigational flaws, participants reacted positively to the gamified nature of the application itself[cite: 277]. This indicates that while the core concept is highly motivating, the UI execution must be refined to reduce friction and better support the end user.
